Watching one account for stock mentions

A monitor for @realDonaldTrump on Truth Social, a platform with no public API.

The problem

Alert whenever he posts about a publicly traded company, by ticker or by name. Two things make that harder than it sounds.

Truth Social offers no API, so getting the posts at all is the first problem. And once you have them, deciding what counts as a stock mention is genuinely ambiguous. He writes in capitals, signs off with his own initials, and talks about markets constantly without ever meaning a stock.

Getting the data

Truth Social runs a fork of Mastodon, and its own web client talks to a Mastodon-shaped JSON endpoint. That endpoint is reachable without an account. The obstacle is Cloudflare, which blocks requests by their TLS fingerprint rather than anything in the request itself.

AttemptResult
curl with a browser user-agent403
curl_cffi impersonating Chrome 124403
curl_cffi impersonating Safari 17200, clean JSON

Two impersonation targets, one works and one does not. I found the working one by trying a few, not by understanding Cloudflare's rules, which is worth being upfront about because it means the approach could stop working without warning.

So there is a fallback. An archive site republishes the same posts over RSS, and it happens to expose the original Truth Social status id for each one. That detail matters more than it sounds: because both sources use the same ids, switching between them cannot cause the agent to re-alert on posts it already sent.

Deciding what counts

This is where most of the work went. The naive version fails immediately on real posts.

Each ticker in the lexicon carries a rating for how ambiguous it is, and the riskier ones have to find supporting finance words nearby before they count. The catch specific to this account is that political and financial vocabulary overlap heavily. Words like trade, market and economy are everyday political language here, so they are treated as weak evidence, while stock, shares and earnings count as strong.

Without that distinction, his habit of writing in capitals turns ordinary words like ALL, BIG and NOW into a constant stream of false alerts, since each is also a real ticker symbol.

Measuring it

Stock mentions are rare, roughly one post in eighty. Sampling 150 posts at random would have turned up almost no positives and made recall impossible to measure, so the evaluation set is stratified, with each group answering a different question.

GroupnQuestion it answers
Candidates23When the system fires, is it right?
Random102What does it miss?
Traps25Do the lookalikes stay quiet?

Every candidate is labeled rather than sampled, so precision is counted exactly instead of estimated. The traps are hand-picked to be difficult, which makes them useful for spotting weaknesses but wrong to include in the headline numbers, so they are reported on their own.

What the numbers mean

Precision, 0.87
Of the alerts it sends, 87% are real. The other 13% are wasted attention.
Recall, 0.74
Of the real mentions, it catches 74%. The rest slip past silently.
Confidence interval, 0.56 to 0.97
The range the true score plausibly sits in. It is wide because only 15 posts in the whole sample were genuine mentions, and small samples give imprecise answers. Reporting a single number here would suggest more certainty than the data supports.
Traps, 25 of 25
Every lookalike was correctly ignored.

The two misses are both explainable. One names S&P Global, which is not in the 95-company lexicon, so it could never have been caught. The other is a bare link with the company name only in the URL, and URLs are deliberately ignored to avoid matching site names.

Almost all of the delay is waiting for the next poll. Detection and delivery are effectively free, so the only thing worth tuning is how often to check, and that trades directly against how much load is placed on someone else's servers.

Being a reasonable guest

The site has no API and no stated permission for this, so the polling is deliberately conservative: at least 2.5 seconds between requests, never more than 600 in an hour, one request at a time, and it waits as long as it is told to after being rate limited. Checking every minute during a busy period and backing off to five minutes when quiet cuts the daily request count by about two thirds while barely affecting how fast alerts arrive.

Labels are model-generatedThe evaluation labels were not produced by hand, which makes the comparison between the rule-based and model-based detectors unreliable. Fixing this needs human labeling. Until then treat every number above as provisional.
